Song Structure: Managing Attention by Section

合抱之木，生于毫末；九层之台，起于累土；千里之行，始于足下。

Laozi, "Dao De Jing", ch. 64

A tree that fills the arms grows from a tiny shoot; a nine-storey terrace rises from a heap of earth; a journey of a thousand li begins with a single step. A whole song grows out of one well-shaped bar. Structure is not slicing a song into blocks. It decides what the listener hears at each second, and at which second they want to keep listening.

Structure is not a formula; it is an energy map

Songwriters often ask “how long should the verse be?” or “does the chorus have to repeat?” Those questions have no answer outside the piece. Structure really solves three things:

  1. Orientation: which section is the listener in now? Melody, harmony and rhythm must give a consistent signal.
  2. Expectation: what does the listener expect before this section ends? The next section should deliver it, or deliberately delay it.
  3. Energy: is this section stronger or weaker than the last? By how much, and through what means?

Draw these three things on a graph with bars on the horizontal axis and energy on the vertical axis, and you have an energy curve. The curve is not a picture for an audience; it is a construction drawing for arranging. It tells you which section needs more parts, which needs space, and which needs a change of texture.

The job of five sections

The most common sections in popular song are intro, verse, pre-chorus{pre-chorus}, chorus, bridge{bridge} and outro{outro}. They do not all have to appear, but each has a clear job.

Section & Main job & Energy position & Where it lands in Yeban \\ Section & Main job & Energy position & Where it lands in Yeban \\ Intro & Establish timbre, tempo and mood; do not spend the chorus's best tricks yet & Low to medium & 4--8 bars; build texture with one or two tracks \\ Verse & Tell the story and give information; keep harmony and rhythm relatively restrained & Low & 8 or 16 bars; melody in a lower-middle register, space in the instrumentation \\ Pre-chorus & Raise expectation and prepare the chorus & Medium, rising & 2--8 bars; add density and harmonic tension, leave a hook in the last bar \\ Chorus & Deliver the most memorable sentence and the fullest harmony & High & 8 or 16 bars; highest lead, most complete instrumentation \\ Bridge & Change the angle and create contrast; may drop down briefly & Low to medium, then lift & 4--8 bars; change chords, groove or timbre; do not write a second verse \\ Outro & Close or fade out; give the listener an ending signal & Medium to low & 4--8 bars; remove layers, extend, return to the intro motif \\

A short song can omit the pre-chorus or bridge; dance, folk and narrative songs also vary section lengths. Do not force a bridge in just to “sound like a pop song”. A section exists because it has a job; if it has no job, do not add it.

When drawing the curve, mark at least four values: how many tracks this section uses, the register of the lead, how dense the rhythm is, and how simple or complex the harmony is. Changing only one of the four usually does not move energy enough; changing all four at once is easy to lose control of. The instrumentation budget in the next section is how you decide “how many of the four to move”.

A practical acceptance line: in common pop/rock writing, a chorus-to-verse RMS difference of about 1.5 dB or more is a rough self-check that the energy actually opens up. It is not a law for every genre; calibrate against reference records and listening. If the difference is too small, add parts or density first; do not just push the master fader.

The instrumentation budget: what to add or remove per section

An instrumentation budget{instrumentation-budget} is a table filled per section, not a table of money. It turns “what should this section add?” from a feeling into a checkable choice.

Section & Drums & Bass & Harmony/pad & Lead & Strings & Percussion \\ Section & Drums & Bass & Harmony/pad & Lead & Strings & Percussion \\ Intro & Light & Optional & 1 layer & Motif & Optional & Accents \\ Verse & Basic & Basic & 1 layer & Vocal/lead & Little & Little \\ Pre-chorus & Denser & Denser & More tension & Rising & Add sustains & Add fill \\ Chorus & Full & Full & 2--3 layers & Highest & Full & Full \\ Bridge & Change groove & Change motion & Change chords & Change role & May remove & May remove \\ Outro & Reduce & Reduce & Extend & Close & Extend & Close \\

Follow two rules when filling the table:

  • Neighbouring sections differ in at least two items. Pushing the drums harder from verse to chorus still sounds like the same section; add a string sustain and move the bass from long notes to eighths, and both density and texture change.
  • Subtract before adding. A bridge is not “add another new instrument group”. Remove half of the chorus first so the ear has space again; then add back in the second half of the bridge.

The budget can live on paper. What matters is whether the Mute/Solo states in the project match it: if the table says “add strings in the chorus” but the strings run from top to bottom, the table is already invalid.

Four ways to join sections

Sections are not finished by simply cutting to the next one. A transition{transition} has four common devices, each with different conditions:

Device & How & When to use it, and the risk \\ Device & How & When to use it, and the risk \\ Hard cut & Change section on a strong beat or bar line without a fill & Strong contrast, clear groove; risk: abruptness -- the previous section must feel finished \\ Fill & Use one bar of drums/bass/guitar to carry the ear into the next section & Verse into chorus, end of pre-chorus; risk: a fill every time becomes tiring, save it for key transitions \\ Harmonic transition & Join sections with a common tone, dominant chord or modulation & Large key or harmonic changes; risk: over-design, the listener only hears “clever” \\ Space / rhythmic bridge & Stop for half a beat, drop the kick, or let the band go quiet before the turn & When the next section must be emphasised; risk: too long a gap sounds like a broken track \\

The pre-chorus deserves its own design: it does not need to be as full as the chorus; it needs to make the listener feel “it is coming”. A common approach is to densify the harmony in the last two bars, lift the melody, add an upward fill, leave the last beat open, and let everything land on the first beat of the chorus.

Realising it in Yeban

To put structure into a Yeban project, work in this order:

  1. Lay out bars on paper first. Use 4- or 8-bar blocks and write the start and end bar of each section. Do not start by writing notes.
  2. Align clips in the arrangement area. The bar ruler and section markers{section-marker} above the arrangement area show bar numbers; place the first clip of each section on the target bar so later copies do not drift.
  3. Use Mute to subtract. Place all layers according to the budget, then mute the layers that should not appear in each section and listen whether the whole really drops. Do not delete tracks outright; deletion makes A/B comparison hard.
  4. Use Solo to check the protagonist. In the chorus, listen especially to the lead and vocal. If the lead cannot stand on its own when soloed, fix the melody; do not cover it with strings.
  5. Watch the overview panel. Yeban's full-song overview panel shows the energy and structure distribution in one view; compare it with the paper curve. Wrong section length or density becomes obvious.
  6. Change the instrumentation after copying. Copying a section (Ctrl+C / Ctrl+V) solves note placement, not “should this section be identical”. After copying, change at least one thing: instrumentation, density or register.

Exercise

Choose a 3--5 minute pop song you know well and complete three tasks:

  1. Count its sections and bars, then draw an energy curve. Mark each section's lead register, drum density and number of harmonic layers.
  2. Build a 32-bar structural skeleton in Yeban: intro 4, verse 8, chorus 8, verse 8, chorus 4. Use only drums, bass, pad and lead; leave detail out.
  3. Use the instrumentation budget to A/B the verse and chorus: mute every layer that “should only be in the chorus”, then add them back one by one. Note which layer makes the contrast clearest and which layer could actually be removed.

Chapter takeaways

  • Structure is an energy map: orientation, expectation and energy.
  • Every section has a job; do not force sections that have no job.
  • Use an energy curve and an instrumentation budget to manage what to add and remove.
  • Neighbouring sections differ in at least two items; subtract before adding.
  • Prefer transition devices over stacked effects; after copying a section, change its instrumentation.
