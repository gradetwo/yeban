Vocal Arrangement: Keep the Lead in Front, Let the Backing Fill Behind

诗者，志之所之也。在心为志，发言为诗。

"Mao Commentary on the Book of Songs", preface

Poetry is where the intent goes: in the heart it is intent; spoken aloud, it becomes poetry. Keep the lead vocal intelligible: every harmony part is a supporting actor. The first rule of vocal arrangement is not “how many layers” but “can the lyric still be heard”. The lead is the protagonist; doubles, harmonies, stacks and ad-libs are supporting roles. If the support steals the scene, the whole section blurs.

Vocals are a cast, not one track

A pop vocal usually contains these roles:

  • Lead vocal: sings the main lyric, carries narrative and emotion; its register, diction and breath must be the clearest.
  • Double: the same lyric sung again and layered on the lead; it is not harmony, it is the same melody.
  • Harmony: sings different notes, usually a third/sixth below the lead, or two high/low lines in the chorus.
  • Stack: the same lyric layered three to five times into a “wall of voices”; common on the final chorus or outro.
  • Ad-lib: a short response, shout or breathy sound at the end of a lead phrase; it carries no lyric information, only colour.

These roles can appear in different sections: verse with lead only, pre-chorus with one double, chorus with harmony, final chorus with stack. Not every section needs the whole cast.

Lead vocal: make the lyric clear first

The first job of lead arrangement is leaving space:

  • Phrase length: no more than four bars per phrase; if longer, split it and leave half a beat to a beat for breath.
  • Register: the lead sits most naturally near the speaking pitch; the chorus may lift it, but do not spend the whole song at the top.
  • Diction: consonants need time; do not squeeze three syllables into one beat. Where the lyric is dense, simplify the melody.
  • Space: leave the end of the lead phrase empty so harmony/ad-libs have somewhere to enter; if the lead fills everything, the support can only fight.

If the lead track is already full, the right move is not more compression but fewer notes or a released phrase ending. Vocal arrangement begins with letting the lead breathe.

Double: the same melody, sung twice

A double{double} is the same lyric sung twice and layered. Its job is not harmony but making the lead thicker and steadier:

  • Layers: none or one in the verse; one in the chorus; two on the final chorus.
  • Recording: a real double needs two takes, not a copied audio clip. Copying the same take makes it louder, not thicker, and may cause phase problems.
  • Alignment: the two takes need not match syllable for syllable; letting consonants offset by a few milliseconds sounds more human; too much offset becomes choir/echo.
  • Register: the double sings the same melody, not an octave below; the low octave belongs to harmony/stack, not double.

If there is only one lead take, it is better to skip the double than to fake thickness with a copied track; a copy is for widening, not for pretending to be a second performance.

Harmony: write a second line under the lead

Vocal harmony{vocal-harmony} differs from instrumental harmony: it is a melodic line the listener can hear, not chord tones scattered across vocal tracks.

  • Interval: the most common is a third or sixth below the lead; a fifth sounds hollow and a second clashes; when unsure, start with a third.
  • Register: harmony usually sits below the lead, not in the same register; the high octave is only for the final chorus phrase.
  • Movement: keep the harmony line smooth; do not leap on every word. When the lead holds a long note, the harmony may move slightly to create tension.
  • Sections: none in the verse, one low harmony in the pre-chorus, two in the chorus (high third/low third); the bridge may keep only one.
  • Lyric: the harmony sings the same lyric, or only vowels; do not put new words in the harmony or the listener will be distracted.

Stack and ad-lib: thickness and colour

Vocal stacking{vocal-stacking} layers the same lyric multiple times into a “wall of voices”. It suits the final chorus, outro or a section that needs ceremony:

  • Layer count: three to five is enough; beyond five the lyric blurs and every layer thins out.
  • Division: two layers sing the lead melody, one a low third, one a high third, one only vowels/breath; do not have all five sing the same words.
  • Pan: keep the lead centred and spread the stack left/right; do not squeeze every layer into the middle.
  • Section: use the stack only on the final chorus or outro; if it starts in the verse, the chorus has nothing “more” to offer.

An ad-lib{ad-lib} is a short response at the end of a lead phrase: an “oh”, a sigh, a shout. It occupies half a beat to two beats in the lead's space; too many ad-libs become a second lead.

Vocal layers across eight bars: lead throughout, double and harmony entering with the chorus, stack only in the last two bars; the verse stays open.

Realising it in Yeban: audio tracks, clips and stems

Vocals are audio, not MIDI; their arrangement in Yeban differs from instruments:

  1. Create audio tracks: use Track $$ + New audio track for lead, double, harmony and stack; do not put every layer on one audio track.
  2. Record or import: use File $$ Real-time recording to WAV... for the lead, or File $$ Import audio file... for existing takes; imported material enters the asset pool before being placed on an audio track.
  3. Trim clips: select an audio clip, drag its edges to change length and drag it to move; leave the lead's phrase ends open instead of trimming everything tight.
  4. Copy and align: if the double/harmony is another take, copy the clip and align it to the lead; do not pass off a copy of the lead as a double. Allow consonants to offset by a few milliseconds.
  5. Use clip mute for arrangement: mute unneeded layers with the clip mute switch rather than deleting them, so the final chorus stack can be opened quickly.
  6. Place with faders and pan: lead centred, double/harmony slightly left/right, stack spread wide; check layer by layer in the mixer (X / F8), not all at once.
  7. Export stems: use File $$ Export stems... to export each vocal layer separately for mixing/mastering; the receipt reports how many tracks were exported and skipped.

Exercise

Build eight bars of “lead + double + harmony + stack”:

  1. Write a four-bar lead phrase, leaving one beat at the end; repeat the second phrase with a lower final note.
  2. Add one double (same melody) in chorus bar 5; add a low-third harmony across bars 5--8.
  3. Add two stack layers in the last two bars: one lead melody, one low third; do not use five layers of the same words.
  4. Use clip mute to make the verse/chorus contrast; export stems and confirm every layer can be found separately.

Finally solo the lead: is every word clear? If removing the harmony/stack makes the lyric clearer, they have already done their supporting job.

Chapter takeaways

  • Vocals are a cast: lead, double, harmony, stack, ad-lib; not one track plus effects.
  • Lead first: phrase length, register, diction and space; make the lyric clear.
  • A double is the same melody recorded twice; a copy is not a double.
  • Harmony is a second melodic line, usually a third/sixth below the lead, moving smoothly.
  • Stack three to five layers with divided roles; use it only in the final chorus/outro.
  • In Yeban, use multiple audio tracks, clip mute, faders/pan and stem export.
