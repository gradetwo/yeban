String Orchestration: Turn Five Parts into One Section

声相应，故生变；变成方，谓之音。

"Book of Rites", "Record of Music"

Sounds answer one another and give rise to change; when change takes patterned form, it is called music. Five string parts answer one another until the texture has shape. Strings sound good not because the timbre is “classy”, but because five parts each have a job: one sings, one pads, one holds the bass, and the inner voices fill the middle.

The string section is a team, not one instrument

The string section{string-section} consists of Violin I, Violin II, Viola, Cello and Double Bass. They differ in register, colour and role; do not write all five tracks as “the same chord”:

  • Violin I: the highest register, usually carrying the lead or the top voice; when the melody is clear, this is the line the listener hears first.
  • Violin II: below Violin I, filling inner voices, doubling the lead an octave lower, or pairing with the viola in a reply.
  • Viola: the mid-frequency glue that connects the high violins and the low cello; darker in colour, suited to inner harmony.
  • Cello: can hold the bass or sing a melody; in the middle register it is often “the second melody”.
  • Double bass: pins the root at the bottom and gives the section a floor; it is not suited to fast melodic lines.

The first step in string orchestration is not choosing a patch but writing a five-row role chart: which part sings the lead, which holds the bass, which fills. Without that chart, five tracks easily become five piano scores fighting each other.

Register: put every part in its comfort zone

The following table gives conservative working ranges, not absolute limits:

Working ranges of the five string parts; the bars show where each part can stand, not a requirement to fill every note.

Wider is not better. Write Violin I below G3 and the colour turns dull; write the double bass above G4 and a short gesture works, but a long line loses support. Put every part back in its comfort zone before discussing harmony.

The skeleton: lead, bass, inner voices

The first layer of string orchestration is three things:

  1. Lead: usually Violin I; in the chorus the cello may take it for a colour change. Do not let all five parts sing the same line.
  2. Bass anchor: cello and double bass hold the root; add the double bass only when more weight is needed. Unison doubling can sound clumsy; the bass may play an octave lower or only on strong beats.
  3. Inner voices: Violin II and viola fill the middle chord tones so the lead and bass do not break apart.

An eight-bar chorus may be assigned like this:

Part & Chorus bars 1--4 & Chorus bars 5--8 \\ Part & Chorus bars 1--4 & Chorus bars 5--8 \\ Violin I & Lead & Lead, final phrase an octave higher \\ Violin II & Thirds/sixths below the lead & Swap inner roles with viola \\ Viola & Chord third/fifth & Short answering phrase \\ Cello & Root and bar-line landings & Root; rises once in the final phrase \\ Double bass & Root on strong beats & Root on strong beats; no weak-beat upstaging \\

This table matters more than “are the chords right”: it decides whether the listener hears one melody with a band behind it, or five lines masking one another.

Spacing: wide at the bottom, close at the top

String voicing{voicing} differs from keyboard voicing: when five parts sound at once, a low register crowded too close turns muddy; a high register spread too wide sounds hollow.

  • Wide low register: leave a fifth or more between cello and double bass so the root and upper parts do not fight.
  • Close high register: keep the intervals among Violin I, Violin II and viola within a third, sixth or fourth; avoid large leaps that open holes.
  • Inner voices yield: do not put an inner voice on the same note and rhythm as the lead; let it enter when the lead rests.
  • Avoid voice crossing: Violin I should not fall below Violin II, and the viola should not sit below the cello for long; crossing confuses who is who.
  • Part count: three parts (lead, inner, bass) are enough; open all five only in the chorus or climax and keep the verse sparse.

Divisi{divisi} is a string-specific device: one section splits into two halves. It enriches harmony but thins every part; if unsure, write three parts first, then open the divisi locally in the chorus.

Bowing and texture: make the strings speak

The same notes with different bowing{bowing} have completely different characters. At least distinguish three:

  • Legato: notes connect without a break; suited to pads, lyrical lines and long chorus phrases. Long legato needs breath; do not write eight bars as one unbroken line.
  • Pizzicato: short and granular, suited to light passages or adding colour to the rhythm section. Do not write it too densely or it becomes a second drum kit.
  • Staccato/short notes: each note starts and stops independently, suited to passages with drive; leave space between short notes or they sound like machine-gun fire.

Tutti{tutti} and chamber-like{chamber-like} are two densities: tutti gives every part a voice, suited to the chorus and climax; chamber-like keeps three parts or fewer, suited to the verse and bridge. Alternating the two densities gives more shape than “thick strings from beginning to end”.

String dynamics are not pulled out with a fader. Shape the “crescendo--diminuendo” with note length, density and the velocity lane; leave only final polish to the mix.

Realising it in Yeban

  1. Create five MIDI tracks: name them Violin I, Violin II, Viola, Cello and Double Bass. Do not put all strings on one track where the parts become invisible.
  2. Load SFZ instruments: use File $$ Load SFZ instrument library... to load a string patch on each track; one part per track, not five parts squeezed into one patch.
  3. Write row by row in the piano roll: press P / F7, write the Violin I lead first, then the cello bass, then the inner voices; change one line at a time.
  4. Use the velocity lane for breath: below the piano roll, draw crescendo/diminuendo into long legato and leave half a beat at phrase ends; do not replace phrasing with a track fader.
  5. Use the automation lane for section changes: expand the automation lane (A) in the arrangement area, lift the strings when the chorus enters and pull them back in the verse; parameter changes are stored in the project and applied on export.
  6. Check balance with Solo/Mute: solo Violin I and confirm the lead stands alone; unmute the inner voices one by one and confirm they do not fight it; finally unmute the rhythm section and confirm the drums do not bury the strings.

Exercise

Write an eight-bar chorus for five string parts:

  1. Write only the Violin I lead and the cello root; confirm the melody and bass can stand on their own.
  2. Add Violin II and viola, filling the chord's third and fifth; inner voices must not share the lead's rhythm.
  3. Let the double bass play the root only on strong beats; then check for voice crossing and a crowded low register.
  4. Finally open tutti in chorus bars 5--8 while keeping three parts in the first four bars; use the velocity lane to give the long legato breath.

When finished, solo each part: which part can be removed without changing the music? That part is the redundant one.

Chapter takeaways

  • The string section is a team of five roles, not one “string patch”.
  • Assign roles first: lead, bass anchor, inner voices; then discuss chords.
  • Put every part in its register; wide at the bottom, close at the top, no voice crossing.
  • Use three parts in the verse and five in the chorus; alternate tutti and chamber-like density.
  • Bowing decides character: legato, pizzicato and short notes each have a job.
  • Realise it in Yeban with five MIDI tracks, SFZ, the velocity lane and automation; the mix is only final polish.
