Melody and Counterpoint: Make One Sentence Memorable, Then Let the Second Answer It

凡音之起，由人心生也。人心之动，物使之然也。

"Book of Rites", "Record of Music"

All music begins in the human heart; when the heart is moved by things, it takes form in sound. Write the sentence you mean first; the melody comes after it. A melody is not better because it has more notes. First make one sentence stand, then decide whether the second repeats, answers or argues.

What a melody must do

The melody layer does three things:

  1. Give identity: the listener must recognise “this is its sentence”, not a replaceable scale exercise.
  2. Give direction: a sentence needs a beginning, a landing and a breath; staying in one register loses direction.
  3. Give emotion: the same lyric feels different when the melody lands in major or minor, high or low, long or short phrases.

A melody does not exist alone. It must land on the harmony's target notes, avoid the rhythm section's accents, and leave breath at section boundaries. Write the sentence first; write the notes second.

Motif: short, memorable, changeable

A motif{motif} is the shortest recognisable unit in a melody, usually 2--5 notes. A good motif is:

  • Short: it can be said within two beats; longer and it becomes a phrase, hard to repeat.
  • Characterful: one leap, one syncopation, one special timbre or velocity makes it memorable.
  • Changeable: it can become a new sentence through transposition, inversion, shortening, extension or rhythmic change, rather than being copied exactly.

Variation & How & Risk \\ Variation & How & Risk \\ Repetition & Play it exactly again & Reinforces memory; three times or more becomes tiresome. \\ Transposition & Move it up or down by several notes & Keeps the contour, changes colour; move too far and it leaves the harmony. \\ Inversion & Reverse the up/down direction & Creates an “answer”; the listener may no longer recognise the original. \\ Shorten/extend & Cut or add notes at the ends & Changes the tone; cut too much and the motif loses its identity. \\ Rhythmic change & Keep the pitches, change durations/accents & Same motif, new character; change too much and it becomes another motif. \\

Choose one feature when writing a motif; do not combine a leap, a syncopation and an ornament at once. More features make it harder to remember and harder to vary.

Phrase: antecedent, consequent, breath

A phrase{phrase} is a melodic unit with a beginning and a landing, commonly 4 or 8 bars. Pop songs most often use an antecedent--consequent pair:

  • Antecedent{antecedent}: poses the question and builds expectation, often ending on an unstable note or half cadence.
  • Consequent{consequent}: answers the question and resolves expectation, often returning to the tonic target note.
  • Breath: leave half a beat to a beat between phrases; without breath, the listener cannot hear where the sentence ends.

A practical check: sing every note of the melody. If you can only breathe after eight bars, the phrase is too long; if you break every two beats, it is too fragmented.

Contour and target notes

Contour{contour} is the melodic shape in pitch: rising, falling, arch, wave, staircase. It decides the listener's intuition about where the sentence is going.

  • Set the contour before filling notes: a whole song circling one register feels flat; a whole phrase rushing upward leaves no room for the chorus.
  • Target notes: each phrase needs at least one target note{target-note} on the chord's root, third, fifth or seventh; the target decides whether the sentence is stable, suspended or resolved.
  • Register management: keep the verse's centre lower and lift it in the chorus; do not spend the highest note in the first phrase.
  • Leaps need a fall: after a large leap (five semitones or more), stepwise motion downward usually settles the line; it is not an absolute rule, but it is reliable.

Motif--variation--contrast--return contour; the dashed counter-line often moves in the opposite direction so the two lines do not rise and fall together.

Rhythm: accents, space and lyric

Melodic rhythm is not “fill notes into the grid”; it is “put the accent on the syllable that needs it”:

  • Lyric stress: the stressed syllable of a multi-syllable word falls on a strong beat or a long note; do not put the accent on a function word.
  • Syncopation: syncopation creates drive, but constant syncopation loses the landing; each phrase needs one clear strong-beat landing.
  • Space: leave gaps between phrases and sections; silence is part of the arrangement.
  • Density contrast: the verse can be sparse and the chorus dense; do not use one density for the whole song.

Counterpoint: how the second sentence answers the first

Counterpoint{counterpoint} is two or more melodic lines moving at once, independent yet coordinated. Pop rarely uses strict counterpoint; three practical relationships matter:

  1. Counter-melody: write an independent line above or below the lead, in a different register, not rhythmically locked; do not copy the lead.
  2. Call-and-response: after the lead phrase, another voice answers with a short phrase, usually 1--2 bars, without stealing the lead's identity.
  3. Contrary motion: the lead rises while the counter-line falls, creating tension; do not keep both lines moving the same way forever.

Device & What it does & Risk \\ Device & What it does & Risk \\ Counter-melody & Different register and rhythm, independent phrase & Same register and rhythm as the lead turns it into a doubled melody. \\ Call-and-response & 1--2 bar answer after the lead & An answer that is too long or too loud steals the lead's identity. \\ Contrary motion & Lines move in opposite directions & Reversing for its own sake makes the harmony unstable. \\

The bottom line of counterpoint is “two roles must be audible”: the lead is the protagonist; the counter-line or answer is the supporting role. If both demand the lead's level, register and density, the listener hears one muddy line.

Realising it in Yeban

  1. Write a single line in the piano roll first. Select a MIDI clip and press P / F7; write only the lead first, not the counter-line at the same time.
  2. Copy the clip for variations. Use Ctrl+C / Ctrl+V to copy a motif clip, then change pitch, rhythm or length; do not rewrite the same motif by hand inside one clip.
  3. Transpose and check target notes. Select notes and transpose the whole phrase; check that each phrase's target note lands on the current chord's root, third, fifth or seventh. Do not lift the whole part an octave just to sound “high”.
  4. Use the velocity lane. Draw velocity below the piano roll; separate the verse and chorus levels and leave breath inside phrases.
  5. Put the counter-line on its own track. Do not stack it inside the lead track; create another MIDI track, keep the register apart, and solo both to check intelligibility.
  6. Check with Solo/Mute. Solo the lead and confirm it stands alone; unmute the counter-line and confirm it does not cover the main phrase; finally unmute the rhythm section and confirm the drums do not eat the accents.

Exercise

Write an eight-bar lead and a four-bar counter-line:

  1. Start with a three-note motif that can be said within two beats; then write variation A' (transpose or change rhythm) and a contrasting B.
  2. Divide the eight bars into a four-bar antecedent and a four-bar consequent. End the antecedent on an unstable note, resolve the consequent to the tonic target note, and leave half a beat of breath between them.
  3. Put the counter-line on a separate track: when the lead rises, the counter-line falls, and the registers stay apart. Solo both lines and confirm they are intelligible together.

Finally, use the velocity lane to make a verse/chorus level difference and note which phrase's target note is clearest.

Chapter takeaways

  • A melody gives identity, direction and emotion before it worries about notes.
  • A motif must be short, memorable and changeable; vary one feature at a time.
  • Phrases have antecedent, consequent and breath; target notes land on chord tones.
  • Set the contour before filling notes; after a leap, stepwise fall usually settles the line.
  • Counterpoint must keep two roles audible: separate register and rhythm, and let the answer stay shorter than the lead.
  • Solve lead/counter-line conflicts at the arranging stage, not with volume.
