Advanced Harmony: Sevenths, Voice Leading and Reharmonisation

What you can do after this chapter

  • Distinguish dominant, major, minor, half-diminished and diminished sevenths by construction and function, instead of treating “seventh chord” as one colour.
  • Check four-part writing with common tones, stepwise motion and resolution, and point out parallel fifths/octaves or an unresolved seventh.
  • Use inversions, drop-2, a pedal bass and sequential reharmonisation to make three versions of one melody.
  • In Yeban, turn an eight-bar I--vi--IV--V into ii--V--I and a borrowed-chord version, then compare bass line and tension.

The five qualities of seventh chords

A seventh chord = triad + seventh. Its quality is fixed by the intervals from the root to the third, fifth and seventh:

Quality & Construction (semitones) & Symbol & Typical position \\ Dominant seventh & 4, 7, 10 & X7 & V7; blues I7/IV7 \\ Major seventh & 4, 7, 11 & Xmaj7 & Imaj7, IVmaj7 \\ Minor seventh & 3, 7, 10 & Xm7 & ii7, vi7, iii7 \\ Half-diminished & 3, 6, 10 & Xm7\(\)5 & viiø7, iiø7 (minor) \\ Diminished seventh & 3, 6, 9 & Xdim7 & passing/symmetric tension, rarely a stable tonic \\

The most common functional cell is ii7--V7--Imaj7: ii7's minor seventh contains the tonic's sixth, V7's tritone (3 and \(\)7) points at I, and Imaj7's major seventh pins the arrival. Do not treat V7's \(\)7 as decoration: it must descend stepwise to the third of I, or the arrival feels suspended.

Voice leading: three hard rules and two soft rules

Four-part writing (SATB or four instruments) reads horizontally first, vertically second. Hard rules:

  1. Resolve the seventh: V7's seventh descends stepwise to the third of I; ii7's seventh descends to the third of V7.
  2. Resolve the leading tone: V7's third (the major leading tone) rises to the tonic of I; in an inner voice it may fall to the fifth, but never leap.
  3. No parallel fifths/octaves: two voices moving in similar motion from one perfect fifth/octave to another weaken independent part-writing.

Soft rules:

  • Prefer common tones, then stepwise motion, then leaps; after a leap, compensate with a step in the opposite direction.
  • Let bass and melody move in contrary motion; avoid simultaneous large leaps in outer voices.
  • Double roots and fifths first; avoid doubling the third (leading tone) and the seventh.

Figure 11-1: Common tones and steps in Cmaj7--Fmaj7--G7--Cmaj7. Keep common tones when possible and move the rest by the smallest step; G7's F falls to Cmaj7's E, B rises to C.

Inversions, drop-2 and the bass line

An inversion changes the bass (third, fifth or seventh), not the chord quality. Fix the bass line first, then choose the inversion:

  • First inversion: third in the bass; useful for stepwise bass (C--E--G--B).
  • Second inversion: fifth in the bass; common as a passing chord or over a pedal.
  • Drop-2: lower the second-highest voice by an octave for a more open keyboard/guitar voicing; check that it does not collide with the bass.

Example: C--F--G--C with bass C--F--G--C makes fourth leaps; C--F/A--G/B--C gives a stepwise C--A--B--C, but do not park F/A's second inversion for long.

Reharmonisation: three ways with the same melody

Reharmonisation is not “replace everything with strange chords”; it changes how the harmony interprets the melody. Three controllable devices:

  1. Functional substitution: I may become vi or iii; IV may become ii; V may become viiø7 or a substitute dominant. The bass and melody must still read as the same function.
  2. Pedal bass: keep the bass fixed and change the upper chords, producing “same root, different colours”; useful in intros and bridges.
  3. Sequential reharmonisation: move the same motivic harmony down/up a second or third while preserving voice relationships; after two sequences, return, or it becomes an exercise.

Original & Substitution & Sound & Risk \\ I--vi--IV--V & ii--V--I--vi & jazzier, descending bass & ii7's seventh must resolve \\ I--IV--V--I & I--IV--ii--V & smoother, delays the tonic & count the ii--V seventh chain \\ I--V--vi--IV & I--V--vi--IV (borrow Fm) & darker, pre-chorus contrast & Fm must return to C \\

In Yeban: split eight bars in half, I--vi--IV--V then ii--V--I--V; solo each bar's bass. If the bass leaps more than a fourth, fix it with inversions before adding colour.

Exercises

  1. Write the four notes of C major V7 and say where the seventh and third resolve.\\

    2. Answer: G B D F; F falls to E, B rises to C.

  2. Why may V7's third fall in an inner voice while the leading tone in an outer voice usually rises?\\

    3. Answer: The outer-voice leading tone has the strongest pull; an inner voice may fall to the fifth to avoid voice crossing, but still may not leap.

  3. Write a stepwise bass for C--F--G--C and mark the inversions.\\

    4. Sample: C--F/A--G/B--C; F in first inversion, G in first inversion.

  4. Change I--vi--IV--V to ii--V--I--vi and name every seventh that must resolve.\\

    5. Answer: ii7's seventh falls to V7's third; V7's seventh then falls to I's third.

  5. Harmonise a four-bar C major melody over a pedal C; write the upper chords.\\

    6. Sample: pedal C, upper Cmaj7--Fmaj7/C--Dm7/C--G7/C.

  6. In Yeban, lay eight bars of I--vi--IV--V with bass C--A--F--G, then change the first half to ii--V--I--vi; compare the bass leaps and seventh resolutions.\\

    7. Sample: the original bass C--A--F--G has third/fourth leaps; the rewrite C--D--C--A is more stepwise, but ii7's seventh must fall to V7's third, not stop on the bar line.
