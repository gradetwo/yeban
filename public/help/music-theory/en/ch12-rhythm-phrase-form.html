Rhythm, Phrase and Form: Advanced

What you can do after this chapter

  • Tell 6/8 from 3/4 by ear and notate a hemiola (three against two) without corrupting the metre.
  • Split an eight-bar passage into motif--development--phrase--cadence and mark the phrase peak and closing beat.
  • Label a 16-bar structure as verse/chorus/bridge and explain each section's rhythmic job, not just its name.
  • In Yeban, make four variations of one motif (sequence, inversion, augmentation, fragmentation) and assemble eight bars.

6/8 is not “six beats of four per bar”

3/4 has three equal beats; 6/8 is a compound metre of two large beats, each containing three eighths. To the ear 3/4 feels like a waltz, 6/8 like a swaying barcarolle. Decide where the “large beat” sits before writing:

Metre & Large beat & Subdivision & Typical use \\ 3/4 & three quarter-note beats & halves/quarters per beat & waltz, three-beat march \\ 6/8 & two large beats (three eighths each) & three eighths per large beat & barcarolle, lyrical chorus, swing \\ 12/8 & four large beats (three eighths each) & slow blues, gospel & triple feel per beat \\

A hemiola temporarily hears “two groups of three” as “three groups of two” (or the reverse). In 6/8, cutting each large beat in half creates a 3/4 illusion; in 3/4, stretching two groups into three each creates a 6/8 illusion. Place a hemiola near a phrase ending to suggest a coming section change.

Groove: accents, syncopation and three-against-two

Groove is not a pattern name; it is the combination of accent cycle, timbre and duration. Three working rules:

  1. Move the accent: push a strong-beat accent earlier or later by half a beat to create syncopation; leave a landing note on the next beat, or the line floats away.
  2. Three against two: in 4/4, let one voice run triplets and another run eighths, producing 3:2; both need a stable period, not randomness.
  3. Leave space: after continuous syncopation, leave half a beat empty so the ear can re-align; syncopating every beat makes the section lose direction.

Sketch an “accent map” first: mark strong, weak, secondary-strong and weak in each bar, then compare melodic accents with drum accents. If every accent lands on the same beat, the groove is flat; if every beat is syncopated, it scatters.

Phrase: motif, peak and cadence

A phrase is a melodic unit that can breathe on its own, usually two to four bars. Analyse three things:

  • Motif: the shortest recognisable figure, usually two to five notes;
  • Phrase peak: the beat of highest tension or densest information, often the top of a sequence;
  • Cadence: how it closes: half cadence (on V), authentic (V--I), plagal (IV--I), deceptive (V--vi).

Four basic ways to develop a motif:

Device & Operation & Aural effect \\ Sequence & move the whole figure up/down & drive, predictability \\ Inversion & reverse interval direction & familiar yet strange \\ Augmentation & stretch durations & emphasis, closing \\ Fragmentation & take only half the motif & acceleration, tension \\

Form: the job of a section, not its label

A common 16-bar structure may be AABA, verse--chorus, or intro--verse--chorus--bridge--outro. The label matters less than each section's rhythmic/harmonic job:

  • Verse: narrative, lower tension, steadier rhythm, often I--vi--IV--V or a loop;
  • Chorus: the hook, denser or higher rhythm, harmony often returns to I, strong cadence at the end;
  • Bridge: contrast, rhythm may slow or speed up, harmony often borrows or modulates;
  • Transition: use hemiola, syncopation or an ascending sequence to carry the ear into the next section.

Eight bars can split as “2+2+4”: two bars establish the motif, two bars sequence/invert it, four bars deliver the peak and cadence. If all eight bars share one rhythmic pattern, it sounds like a loop; if the metre changes every two bars, it sounds like an exercise.

Exercises

  1. 6/8 and 3/4 both contain six eighths; why do they sound different?\\

    2. Answer: 6/8 groups them into two large beats of three eighths; 3/4 has three equal quarter beats; the accent cycle differs.

  2. Write a two-bar 4/4 rhythm whose second bar implies 3/4 through hemiola.\\

    3. Sample: bar 1 evenly four beats; bar 2 groups eighths as 3+3+2.

  3. Apply sequence, inversion and augmentation to the four-note motif “1 2 3 5”.\\

    4. Sample: sequence 2 3 4 6; inversion 1 7 6 4 (axis 1); augmentation 1 1 2 2 3 3 5 5 (double durations).

  4. Analyse an eight-bar passage and mark its peak and cadence type.\\

    5. Answer: the peak is usually the sequence top or highest note; read the last two bars' bass and chords: V--I authentic, V--vi deceptive.

  5. Why leave space after continuous syncopation?\\

    6. Answer: so the listener can re-align to the beat; syncopation with no landing loses direction.

  6. In Yeban, make sequence, inversion, augmentation and fragmentation of “1 2 3 5” and assemble eight bars; at least one variation must change metre or add a hemiola.\\

    7. Sample: bars 1--2 original, 3--4 sequence, 5--6 inversion, 7--8 augmentation plus fragmentation; bar 8 closes with a 3+3+2 hemiola.
